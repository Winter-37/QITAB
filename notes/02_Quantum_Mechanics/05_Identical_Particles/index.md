Two-particle systems, the symmetrization principle and the exchange "force"; helium and the periodic table as the first many-electron problems; the free electron gas and band structure (Griffiths Ch. 5). This is the bridge to Slater determinants in Part 4.

## What this chapter is about

Two electrons cannot be told apart. Exchanging them can therefore change the wave function at most by a sign, and nature uses both signs:

$$
\psi(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2) = \pm\,\psi(\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_1).
$$

Particles that take the $+$ sign are bosons (integer spin), and those that take the $-$ sign are fermions (half-integer spin). This chapter works out the consequences:

- the Pauli exclusion principle;
- the exchange "force", an effective attraction or repulsion produced by symmetry alone;
- how spin and spatial symmetry are tied together for electrons;
- the first many-electron atoms;
- the electron gas, as the simplest model of a metal.

## Why it matters

- **Antisymmetry is the defining feature of electronic structure.** Slater determinants, exchange integrals, Fermi holes and the exchange operator $\hat K$ in Hartree–Fock all follow from the minus sign above. Without it there is no shell structure and no periodic table.
- **Spin and space are coupled.** For two electrons the total wave function must be antisymmetric. A singlet spin state therefore pairs with a symmetric spatial function, and a triplet with an antisymmetric one. Different spatial symmetry means different average electron–electron repulsion. This is the textbook explanation of singlet–triplet splittings and of Hund's first rule, even though the Hamiltonian contains no spin.
- **Helium shows why the many-electron problem is hard.** Dropping the electron–electron repulsion gives an energy far too low. Every improvement, whether perturbative, variational or screened, is a first step toward the correlation methods of Part 4.
- **The electron gas underlies density functional theory.** The uniform gas, with its Fermi energy $\propto\rho^{2/3}$, is the reference system for the local density approximation.

## The main ideas, in order

1. **Two particles.** The two-particle wave function, separation into centre-of-mass and relative motion, the reduced mass, and the first example of entanglement.
2. **Symmetrization.** Bosons and fermions, the Pauli principle, and the exchange force as a change in $\langle(x_1-x_2)^2\rangle$.
3. **With spin.** The exchange operator, and the pairing of spin singlet with spatially symmetric and triplet with spatially antisymmetric states.
4. **Helium.** The independent-particle model, parahelium and orthohelium, and first-order repulsion.
5. **The periodic table.** Aufbau with screening, configurations, term symbols and Hund's rules.
6. **Solids.** The free electron gas (Fermi energy, degeneracy pressure) and Bloch's theorem with band gaps from the Dirac comb.

Symmetry operators (translation, parity, rotation, time reversal), how they give conservation laws, degeneracy and selection rules, and the Wigner–Eckart theorem; then the Heisenberg picture and the path integral (Griffiths Ch. 6, with Sakurai companions).

## What this chapter is about

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the Hamiltonian unchanged. In quantum mechanics it is represented by an operator $\hat T$ with $[\hat H,\hat T]=0$. For a continuous symmetry the operator is generated by an observable:

- momentum generates translations, $\hat T(a)=e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar}$;
- angular momentum generates rotations;
- the Hamiltonian generates time translation.

This chapter builds those operators and draws out the three consequences of a symmetry:

- a **conserved quantity**;
- **degeneracy**, when several symmetry operators do not commute with each other;
- **selection rules**, which make whole classes of matrix elements vanish.

It closes with two reformulations of dynamics: the Heisenberg picture and Feynman's path integral.

## Why it matters

- **Symmetry is free information.** Before any calculation it says which states are degenerate, which quantum numbers are good, and which integrals are zero. The degeneracy of hydrogen ($n^2$, larger than rotational symmetry alone explains) and Kramers' degeneracy of odd-electron systems both come from symmetry arguments.
- **The Wigner–Eckart theorem** factorizes every matrix element of a tensor operator into a geometric Clebsch–Gordan coefficient times a single reduced matrix element. Spin coupling, spectroscopic intensities and spin-adapted electronic-structure methods use it constantly.
- **Spin ½ is double-valued.** SU(2) covers SO(3) twice, so a $2\pi$ rotation multiplies a spinor by $-1$. This was confirmed by neutron interferometry and is a direct consequence of the group structure.
- **Time reversal is antiunitary.** For spinless particles it reduces to complex conjugation. That is why real orbitals can be chosen for a real (field-free, spin-free) Hamiltonian, and why complex and generalized Hartree–Fock solutions are classified by these symmetries.
- **Different pictures, same physics.** The Heisenberg picture is how second-quantized operators and propagators are written. The path integral connects quantum mechanics with classical action and with statistical mechanics.

## The main ideas, in order

1. **Translation and parity.** Generators, unitary transformations of operators, Bloch's theorem, and Laporte's rule that dipole transitions connect states of opposite parity.
2. **Rotation.** $\hat R_{\mathbf n}(\phi)=e^{-i\phi\,\mathbf n\cdot\hat{\mathbf L}/\hbar}$, vector operators, and the Sakurai companions: finite rotations and SU(2) versus SO(3), Wigner $D$ matrices, and Schwinger's two-oscillator construction.
3. **Conservation and degeneracy.** Commuting with $\hat H$ conserves the full outcome distribution of an observable. Non-commuting symmetries force degeneracy (the Runge–Lenz vector, Kramers' theorem).
4. **Time reversal.** It must be antiunitary, $\Theta=UK$, and $\Theta^2=-1$ for half-integer spin.
5. **Selection rules.** For scalar and vector operators, and then in general through spherical tensors and the Wigner–Eckart theorem.
6. **Dynamics reformulated.** The time-evolution operator and the Heisenberg picture; the propagator and the path integral.

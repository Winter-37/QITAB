Nonrelativistic quantum mechanics of one and a few particles, from the postulates to the approximation methods. The spine is Griffiths (3rd ed.); Sakurai companion notes deepen the formalism and the treatment of symmetry.

## What this part is about

The first three chapters set up the theory: what the wave function means, how it evolves, and the general language of Hilbert spaces and Hermitian operators. Chapters 4–6 apply it to the structures that recur throughout chemistry. These are the hydrogen atom and angular momentum, spin and identical particles, and the symmetries that fix degeneracies and selection rules. Very few problems can be solved exactly, so Chapters 7–11 are about approximation:

- variational bounds;
- perturbation series;
- the semiclassical (WKB) limit;
- scattering from a potential;
- dynamics under time-dependent fields.

## Why it is here

Electronic-structure theory (Part 4) is quantum mechanics applied to electrons in the field of fixed nuclei. Almost every idea it uses is introduced here first, in a setting small enough to calculate by hand:

- **Hilbert space and operators** (Chapter 3) → matrix representations, basis sets and the eigenvalue problems of Hartree–Fock and CI.
- **Angular momentum and spin** (Chapters 4 and 6) → atomic orbitals, spin coupling, term symbols and spin-adapted configurations.
- **Antisymmetry** (Chapter 5) → Slater determinants, exchange and the Pauli principle.
- **The variational principle** (Chapter 7) → Hartree–Fock, CI and MCSCF, all of which minimize an energy over a restricted space.
- **Perturbation theory** (Chapter 8) → Møller–Plesset theory and the response properties of Part 4, Chapter 16.
- **Time-dependent theory** (Chapter 11) → spectroscopy, transition rates and linear response.
- **Density operators** (Chapter 3) → reduced density matrices (Part 4, Chapter 6) and quantum information (Part 5).

## The storyline

| Chapters | Stage | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Foundations | What is a quantum state, how is it measured, and how does it evolve? |
| 4–6 | Structure | What do rotational invariance, spin and particle identity imply for spectra? |
| 7–8 | Stationary approximations | How do we get energies when $\hat H$ cannot be diagonalized exactly? |
| 9–10 | Semiclassics and the continuum | What happens near the classical limit, and for unbound states? |
| 11 | Dynamics | How does a system respond to a time-dependent perturbation? |

## How to read it

**Full route.** Read the chapters in order; the Previous/Next buttons follow it.

**Electronic-structure route.** Chapters 1–5, then 7 and 8, then Part 4. Return to Chapter 6 (rotation groups, Wigner–Eckart theorem, time reversal) when spin coupling and symmetry become important. Return to Chapter 11 for spectroscopy and response. Chapters 9 and 10 can be left for later.

The mathematics these chapters need is collected in Part 0: linear algebra (Chapter 1), Fourier transforms and the delta function (Chapter 2), and special functions (Chapter 3).
